# Validate CoastSnap Image Alignment Reliably

The most defensible numerical test is **target registration error (TRE) on stable landmarks that were withheld from fitting**, reported across the image and across diverse CoastSnap captures. A low fitting residual, a high image-similarity score, or a visually convincing overlay can all coexist with a wrong transformation. Since CoastSnap images come from different phones and capture conditions and contain a changing shoreline, registration quality should be tested on persistent land features, while shoreline accuracy should be evaluated separately against contemporaneous survey data. The recommended evaluation is therefore a small panel of complementary measures, not one blended score: held-out geometric error as the headline, robustness and coverage as operational measures, and an end-to-end shoreline comparison to show whether the alignment is good enough for its scientific purpose. No published CoastSnap standard establishes a universal alignment threshold; set it from the ground-scale change the project needs to resolve.

## Held-out landmarks measure geometric alignment directly

For each source and target pair, annotate corresponding points that are physically stable and visible in both images, such as corners of permanent structures, fixed markers, or distinctive rock features. Split these into points used to estimate or tune the transform and **independent check points used only for scoring**. If a source point is mapped to the target by the estimated transform, its TRE is the Euclidean distance between the mapped location and its manually marked target location. Score the check points, never the points used by the homography, optimizer, or RANSAC fit: fitting residuals measure how well the transform explains its inputs, not how well it predicts unseen locations. This distinction is the same reason remote-sensing accuracy standards require independent checkpoints ([USGS JACIE best practices](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/ofr20241023/full), [ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards](https://aagsmo.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/ASPRS_PosAcc_Edition2_MainBody.pdf)).

Distribute check points across the full usable field of view, including edges and the near, middle, and far parts of the beach. A cluster near the center can report a low average while leaving rotation, scale, lens distortion, or perspective errors undetected elsewhere. Exclude the current waterline, wet/dry boundary, swash, foam, sandbars, moving vegetation, people, clouds, and shadows as reference points: these can change between photos even when the camera alignment is correct. CoastSnap submissions use different smartphone cameras, resolutions, crops, and compression, while sandbars and beach state evolve with tide and waves; these scene differences make a stable-land test essential ([NSW CoastSnap overview](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/research-and-publications/your-research/citizen-science/get-involved/coastsnap), [Harley & Kinsela, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csr.2022.104796), [SCShores dataset paper](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)).

Report TRE as a distribution, not just a single average: signed horizontal and vertical bias, median, mean absolute error, RMSE, 90th or 95th percentile, maximum, and the proportion below predeclared tolerances. Include residual-vector plots and errors by image region. Median/MAE describe typical performance robustly; RMSE gives more weight to large misses; a tail percentile and maximum expose occasional poor registrations. A hit rate answers an operational question such as “what share of the beach area is within tolerance?” A tolerance should reflect annotation repeatability and the downstream need, not a generic image-registration convention. Have a second annotator mark a subset, or repeat annotations, to quantify reference-point uncertainty; do not imply that hand-marked coordinates are exact. For comparisons across image resolutions, normalized errors can supplement pixel errors, but they do not replace physical units where those matter.

CoastSnap’s oblique views make one target-image pixel correspond to different ground distances at different positions. Convert check-point errors to ground units using the local site camera model or image-to-ground mapping, and report where the conversion applies. The CoastSnap Toolbox already offers a useful pattern: it computes pixel reprojection RMSE for selected ground control points and can plot non-selected points as checks ([CoastSnap Toolbox rectification code](https://github.com/Coastal-Imaging-Research-Network/CoastSnap-Toolbox/blob/master/GUI/CSPGrectifyImage.m)). But fitted-control RMSE remains a fit diagnostic unless the points were excluded from all fitting. CoastSnap studies show why spatial reporting matters: one North Narrabeen assessment reported shoreline cross-shore RMSE of 1.4 m near the camera and 3.9 m over nearly 1 km, while SCShores reported large variation in ground pixel footprint with site and distance ([Harley et al.](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs10111744), [González-Villanueva et al.](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)). These are downstream shoreline results, not registration error thresholds.

## Similarity and overlap are useful secondary signals

When manually labeled correspondences are scarce, image-based metrics can help flag gross failures or compare before and after under consistent conditions. They are **proxies for appearance agreement, not direct evidence of geometric correctness**. Mean squared error (MSE) and peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) are sensitive to brightness and contrast changes; normalized cross-correlation (NCC) and structural similarity (SSIM) are more tolerant to some photometric variation; mutual information (MI) is useful when intensity relationships differ. None has a universal scale that converts to “pixels of alignment error.” Research has shown that similarity and overlap scores can remain favorable for intentionally inaccurate registrations, so they should not be the sole evaluation or pass/fail criterion ([Rohlfing, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3274625/), [Czolbe et al.](https://backend.orbit.dtu.dk/ws/files/320834381/1_s2.0_S1361841523000907_main.pdf)).

If you use these measures, mask to stable land regions and report sea, sky, and shoreline regions separately or exclude them. Waves, glare, moving water, cloud cover, shadows, and real beach change can dominate a whole-image score and reward a visually similar but geometrically wrong match. Use the same masks, grayscale or gradient representation, resampling, and overlap area for every pipeline variant. Compare each score with the unaligned baseline, and on a labeled subset, test whether a change in the score actually tracks a change in held-out TRE. If no such relationship has been demonstrated for CoastSnap imagery, describe it as a quality-control signal only.

Different geometric and contour metrics answer different questions. Symmetric transfer or reprojection error checks how matched features behave under the transform in both directions, but an inlier-only value is conditional on the feature detector and matching process and is not independent accuracy. Report the number and spatial coverage of matches alongside it ([Hartley & Zisserman, *Multiple View Geometry*](https://www.changjiangcai.com/files/text-books/Richard_Hartley_Andrew_Zisserman-Multiple_View_Geometry_in_Computer_Vision-EN.pdf)). If the task is to align a known static boundary or region, Dice/IoU can describe mask overlap, while average surface distance and a robust tail measure such as HD95 describe boundary displacement. A global overlap score can hide local errors, and maximum Hausdorff distance can be dominated by one outlier. These can supplement, not replace, landmark TRE. For a deformable warp, also inspect warp magnitude, smoothness, folds/Jacobian anomalies, and inverse consistency; these are plausibility checks, not proof that the mapped points are correct ([registration metrics review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.imu.2024.101540), [Rohlfing, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3274625/)).

## Reliability requires failures and scene variation in the score

A pipeline that aligns only easy images can show excellent conditional accuracy and still be unreliable in practice. Keep every attempted pair in the denominator and report coverage (the share accepted as successful), rejection/failure rate, and catastrophic-misalignment rate alongside TRE on accepted pairs. Include difficult but valid examples and examples that genuinely lack enough stable features; a safe rejection is different from returning a plausible but wrong transform. CoastSnap acquisition regimes range from standard stations to DIY or free-roaming images, and fixed cradles still accommodate different contributor phones and image processing ([Harley & Kinsela, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csr.2022.104796), [SCShores dataset paper](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)).

Build the test corpus across sites, dates, phone models when known, image dimensions and aspect ratios, compression, lighting, weather, occlusion, and feature density. Separate model development and tuning from final testing by holding out whole dates, devices, or stations; randomly splitting points or near-duplicate images risks leakage. Report each important stratum and image-region result, because one pooled number can conceal failures for distant beach areas or low-feature images. For stochastic methods, rerun with different seeds and modest input perturbations and report transform variability. This measures repeatability, not absolute accuracy: all runs can consistently produce the same biased answer. When there are enough independent images, compare pipeline variants on the same held-out pairs and calculate uncertainty by resampling at the pair/date level rather than treating every point from one pair as an independent trial. Standards such as ASPRS recommend independent checkpoints and broad distribution; the commonly cited 30-checkpoint guidance is a useful target for geospatial product assessments when feasible, not a universal minimum for each CoastSnap image pair ([ASPRS Positional Accuracy Standards](https://aagsmo.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/ASPRS_PosAcc_Edition2_MainBody.pdf), [USGS UAS calibration guidance](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/ofr20231033/full)).

## Validate shoreline outcomes in a separate end-to-end test

Alignment-only validation answers whether stable scene points land in the right target-image positions. It does not establish that the complete CoastSnap workflow produces accurate shoreline positions. For that, run registered images through the same rectification and shoreline extraction steps used operationally, then compare the resulting shoreline with temporally close RTK-GNSS/DGPS measurements along fixed transects or with an appropriate point-to-line distance. Report signed cross-shore bias, MAE or MAD, RMSE/RMSD, median and upper percentiles, and the fraction within a tolerance chosen for the monitoring objective. Record survey time offset, tide, wave conditions, shoreline proxy/definition, and the range or transect because a dynamic shoreline can move independently of registration. Do not mix this end-to-end score with TRE; the former includes camera calibration, rectification, tide, shoreline interpretation, and survey timing as well as alignment error ([SCShores dataset paper](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023), [NSW CoastSnap overview](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/research-and-publications/your-research/citizen-science/get-involved/coastsnap)).

Published CoastSnap shoreline validations are useful context, but they are not alignment benchmarks. SCShores compared shorelines with temporally matched RTK-GNSS waterlines and reported overall MAD of 2.76 m, RMSD of 3.71 m, and 67.45% of errors at or below 3 m, with substantial station-to-station variation; its authors discuss registration and resolution among several contributors ([González-Villanueva et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)). Another CoastSnap overview reported a 5.4 m RMSE at a nearby Bombo/Coledale transect for the 2020 shoreline series ([Harley & Kinsela, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csr.2022.104796)). Differences in site, shoreline definition, tide, waves, and processing mean these numbers should not be used as a universal alignment pass threshold. They demonstrate why a pipeline intended for shoreline science needs both a direct registration test and a separate end-to-end check.

## A practical benchmark produces a decision, not just a score

Start with a curated, versioned set of source/target pairs representing routine and challenging CoastSnap conditions. Have stable correspondences marked and adjudicated for a representative subset, then reserve spatially distributed points in each pair for scoring only. Keep a final holdout split by date/device/site untouched during development. For every method, report held-out TRE in pixels and local ground units, signed bias, median/mean and RMSE, 95th percentile and maximum, residual maps, threshold hit rates, and failure coverage. Break results down by site, device/resolution, condition, and region of the image. Compare variants on identical pairs and report pair-level uncertainty. On a separate, representative subset, run the entire rectification and shoreline workflow and compare with near-coincident RTK-GNSS/DGPS surveys.

If no correspondence ground truth currently exists, use the best available controls first: stable surveyed GCPs or carefully annotated persistent landmarks, with an independent portion withheld. Until that set is built, use masked stable-land NCC/SSIM or edge alignment, symmetric transfer error, warp plausibility, and repeated-run stability as provisional diagnostics only; label the result “proxy QC,” not measured accuracy. Even a modest manually checked benchmark can calibrate these proxies and reveal whether a pipeline variant’s apparent improvement corresponds to smaller geometric error. Set the acceptance rule before the final test from the smallest shoreline change the monitoring program needs to resolve and the local pixel-to-ground scale. That turns the benchmark into a clear decision: where the pipeline is accurate enough, where it needs to reject or flag images, and where additional controls or a different model are needed.

## Conclusion

CoastSnap’s changing shoreline makes whole-image similarity a particularly weak stand-in for alignment quality. The strongest evidence is independent geometric agreement on stable land, sampled across the view and across the conditions in which the pipeline will be used. A second, separately reported comparison to field-surveyed shorelines establishes whether that geometric performance translates into useful coastal measurements; neither score should conceal the other.
