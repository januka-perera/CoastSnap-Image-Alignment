# CoastSnap scene and workflow context

## What CoastSnap images and the alignment task look like

### Takeaway
CoastSnap images come from a nominally fixed viewpoint, but not a calibrated single camera: the cradle fixes the phone position and viewing direction while contributors bring different phones, lenses, resolutions, image crops, and degrees of social-media compression. The alignment target is therefore a static-scene registration problem embedded in a much less static coastal scene; stable land infrastructure should anchor quality assessment, while shoreline/water pixels should not be treated as invariant alignment evidence.

### Cited Findings
- CoastSnap uses photo-point cradles to ensure submissions are captured from the same location; its processing normalizes images from different smartphone cameras and accounts for tide when mapping shorelines. [NSW Environment and Heritage CoastSnap overview](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/research-and-publications/your-research/citizen-science/get-involved/coastsnap)
- The CoastSnap overview explains that beach sand bars migrate and change shape in response to waves and tides, while storms can move sand offshore and calmer conditions return it. These are genuine scene changes, not registration errors. [NSW Environment and Heritage CoastSnap overview](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/research-and-publications/your-research/citizen-science/get-involved/coastsnap)
- CoastSnap dissemination imagery uses registration/image stabilization to address smartphone lens characteristics, different image resolutions, and camera-position variation in the cradle. The published CoastSnap overview says Photoshop auto-align has been used operationally; scientific shoreline products instead rectify using fixed GCPs. [Harley & Kinsela, 2022, CoastSnap overview](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csr.2022.104796)
- In the SCShores CoastSnap workflow, images are registered to a station target image and standardized to its resolution. Social platforms and the CoastSnap app may reduce submitted image widths to 1080, 1200, and 1920 pixels respectively; greater resolution disparity requires more interpolation and increases uncertainty in the resulting pixel locations. [González-Villanueva et al., 2023, SCShores dataset](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)
- A CoastSnap processing paper identifies image registration and resolution, the number/distribution/suitability of GCPs, tide bias, beach morphology, station location/distance/orientation, pixel footprint, shadows, and wave conditions as contributors to shoreline extraction error. [González-Villanueva et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)
- The official CoastSnap Toolbox uses a camera model with field camera position fixed from surveyed station coordinates, estimates camera orientation and optimizes focal length against GCP image coordinates, and reports reprojection RMSE in pixels. It plots GCPs omitted from the fit for a separate accuracy check, offering a useful pattern for independent check points rather than relying only on fitted residuals. [CoastSnap Toolbox rectification code](https://github.com/Coastal-Imaging-Research-Network/CoastSnap-Toolbox/blob/master/GUI/CSPGrectifyImage.m)

### Inferences
- Evaluate registration on persistent terrain/buildings/structures/rock outcrops (and independently surveyed GCPs if available), not on wet/dry lines, swash, surf, foam, shadows, clouds, people, vegetation that moves, or sandbars. Those features change between submissions and can make pixel similarity reward an incorrect alignment.
- Separate the assessment of image-to-target registration from downstream georectification and shoreline accuracy. Pixel reprojection error on stable landmarks diagnoses alignment; shoreline distance against coincident RTK-GNSS or surveyed shorelines diagnoses the full science product. Both are needed, but they answer different questions.
- Report alignment errors both in target-image pixels and as spatial ground displacement by propagating the camera/rectification geometry. A single pixel threshold is misleading when pixel footprints expand with distance and vary by camera height, field of view, source resolution, and shoreline position.

### Gaps
- The CoastSnap publications reviewed describe the auto-alignment/stabilization process but do not publish a standardized, standalone benchmark of registration accuracy across the range of phones, sites, and capture failures. Most published accuracy figures are for final shoreline positions and confound registration with calibration, rectification, tide, shoreline definition, and image resolution.

## What numerical accuracy studies establish for CoastSnap

### Takeaway
Published CoastSnap validation is mostly end-to-end shoreline comparison against in-situ GPS/RTK-GNSS, rather than an isolated alignment score. These studies show that coastal scene geometry and environmental conditions materially alter spatial error; report error distributions by site, alongshore/cross-shore position, range from the camera, and conditions instead of one pooled number.

### Cited Findings
- The original CoastSnap pilot at North Narrabeen reported shoreline cross-shore RMSE of 1.4 m near the camera and 3.9 m over almost 1 km of beach compared with in-situ surveys; image-resolution differences (about 0.3 MP compressed social images versus over 2 MP originals) had only marginal impact on reported shoreline accuracy in that pilot. [Holman et al. review citing Harley et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs10111744)
- A 2022 CoastSnap overview reports comparison of the 2020 Bombo/Coledale-derived shorelines with RTK-GPS at a nearby transect as RMSE = 5.4 m over the year. This is an end-to-end shoreline result, not an image-registration-only error. [Harley & Kinsela, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csr.2022.104796)
- SCShores validated mapped CoastSnap shorelines against temporally matched RTK-GNSS waterlines (average time offset ±11.4 minutes), using nearest Euclidean distances between mapped and surveyed shoreline points. It reported MAD, RMSD, 75th percentile (Q3), and percentage of errors at or below 3 m (P3): all-site MAD 2.76 m, RMSD 3.71 m, Q3 3.78 m, P3 67.45%; accuracy varied by station (MAD 1.68–5.95 m, P3 20–86%). [González-Villanueva et al., 2023, methods and Table 7](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)
- SCShores reported large site- and distance-dependent ground pixel footprints; for its examples the longshore footprint at 450 m ranged from 3.98 m/pixel at the best site to 21.67 m/pixel at the lowest-elevation site. It excluded beyond-site thresholds (150–450 m) from validated shoreline segments where resolution, curvature, slope, GCP distribution, or wave shadows made mapping unreliable. [González-Villanueva et al., 2023, Tables 3–4](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)
- SCShores found about 67% of errors ≤3 m and 75% below 3.8 m overall; its authors identify registration/resolution, resection/rectification/GCP quality, tide bias, morphology, and camera-view geometry as error contributors. [González-Villanueva et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)
- A 2023 U.S. Army Engineer Research and Development Center report found CoastSnap shorelines compared well with RTK and lidar under low-to-moderate waves (RMSE <10 m); high-wave conditions had RMSE up to 18 m, improved when wave run-up was incorporated. [USACE ERDC report notice and abstract](https://www.erdc.usace.army.mil/Media/Publication-Notices/Tag/256998/beaches-monitoring/)
- A 2026 CoastSnap shoreline-extraction study reports validation against RTK-GNSS with overall MAD 2.76 m (site range 1.68–5.95 m); its abstracted methods state that images are registered to a single target before bulk rectification. This repeats similar SCShores accuracy figures and may use overlapping data/workflow, so it should not be treated as independent replication without checking the paper's dataset lineage. [2026 Environmental Modelling & Software article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2026.107078)

### Inferences
- A good alignment pipeline should be judged on independently held-out, spatially distributed static landmarks, but a second system-level check should propagate aligned images through rectification and compare shoreline positions to near-coincident ground truth. Use both median/MAE-type and RMS-type error plus tail/threshold statistics; RMSE alone can hide location-specific failures and is sensitive to outliers.
- Include alongshore/cross-shore and distance-from-station stratification, phone/source-resolution strata, and conditions (tide, light, waves, shadows, foreground occlusion). The reported CoastSnap results show that the same nominal workflow can deliver materially different ground accuracy by location and environment.
- For CoastSnap, the practical “good enough” threshold should be tied to the spatial resolution of the orthorectified output and the intended shoreline-change signal. Evaluating pixel alignment alone cannot establish that errors are small relative to the ground-scale shoreline change of interest.

### Gaps
- The sources do not supply a universal pass/fail threshold for alignment residuals in target pixels. This is expected because the same pixel residual can correspond to different metre errors at different locations in an oblique view.
- Several accuracy studies compare shoreline positions under different shoreline definitions (instantaneous swash edge, waterline, wet/dry proxy) and environmental conditions. Cross-study metre RMSEs are therefore not directly interchangeable.

## How published and available workflows should inform a registration benchmark

### Takeaway
Build an alignment-only benchmark around stable controls that the algorithm does not use for fitting, then report end-to-end shoreline effects separately. CoastSnap’s own photogrammetry tooling already exposes fitted GCP reprojection RMSE and can plot GCPs excluded from the calibration fit; however, the literature emphasizes that an apparently low fitted RMSE can still coexist with a poor geometric fit when controls are unsuitable or poorly distributed.

### Cited Findings
- The CoastSnap Toolbox selects GCPs, digitizes their image pixel coordinates, optimizes a camera model using known GCP world coordinates, estimates focal length by minimizing pixel-space MSE, computes pixel RMSE, and plots any non-selected GCPs as check points. It also applies a configurable maximum RMSE save threshold. [CoastSnap Toolbox code](https://github.com/Coastal-Imaging-Research-Network/CoastSnap-Toolbox/blob/master/GUI/CSPGrectifyImage.m)
- The toolbox README identifies itself as the CoastSnap community beach monitoring code repository and directs users to the original Coastal Engineering paper for technical detail. [CoastSnap Toolbox repository](https://github.com/Coastal-Imaging-Research-Network/CoastSnap-Toolbox)
- The 2024 CoastSnap guide indexed by the search engine warns that a lower RMSE alone does not always mean a better fit; its example reports a lower RMSE for a fit with poorly matched measured/estimated controls, while removing two problematic controls produces better apparent geometry despite a larger RMSE. [Soriano-González & Sánchez-García, 2024 guide](https://doi.org/10.25704/syct-r048)
- The SCShores study used expert review/editing after automatic shore detection because the sand-water boundary is not always unambiguous; shadows, wet areas, wave conditions, and beach morphology affected interpretability. [González-Villanueva et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-15-4613-2023)
- NSW’s CoastSnap guidance confirms that the shoreline product accounts for tide level, and that beach state changes with wave/tide conditions. [NSW Environment and Heritage CoastSnap overview](https://www.environment.nsw.gov.au/research-and-publications/your-research/citizen-science/get-involved/coastsnap)

### Inferences
- For a fair method comparison, split labeled landmark correspondences by capture/site (not random pixels within the same image) into registration/training, tuning, and untouched test sets. Keep a subset of stable landmarks entirely out of transform estimation and compute median, mean/MAE, RMSE, 90th/95th percentile, and success rate below a predefined pixel and ground-distance threshold.
- Because CoastSnap permits phone and image variability, stratify test scores by phone model if known, source resolution, compression, image crop/aspect ratio, orientation, and capture quality. Avoid duplicate frames from the same date/site crossing train/test boundaries.
- Audit residual vector maps for spatial pattern and extrapolation. A small pooled residual can conceal systematic rotation, scale drift, lens-distortion mismatch, or large errors at distant shoreline regions; use spatially distributed check points and report error versus image position / range.
- Add a downstream validation set with paired, time-matched RTK shoreline surveys: compare signed cross-shore error along matched transects or point-to-line distances, MAE/MAD, RMSE, median and 75th/95th percentiles, and threshold hit rates. Keep time difference and shoreline proxy explicit; match tide/wave conditions or quantify their effect.

### Gaps
- I did not find a published CoastSnap study that isolates image-registration performance across diverse smartphone models using a public set of independent manually labeled static-landmark correspondences. Available CoastSnap figures mainly quantify rectification or final shoreline accuracy, so a benchmark should define and publish this missing ground truth explicitly.
- The manual guide's RMSE example was surfaced in search results but the PDF could not be inspected line-by-line in this research pass; treat its specific example as supporting context and consult the guide directly before quoting its numbers in a formal methods section.
